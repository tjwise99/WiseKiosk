WiseKiosk · display language · ticket 132

It is not a screen.
It is a heads-up display on a mirror.

Every design instinct that works on a monitor is wrong here, and the reason is one physical fact: the panel can only add light. It can never darken what is behind a glyph, because what is behind a glyph is the room. This page records what the deployed kiosk measurably is, what a photograph of it in situ proved that no measurement could, and the properties its replacement owes.

The governing constraint

additive only

Black is not a colour on this display. It is transparency.

An unlit pixel is a mirror, so the ground behind every character is whatever the room reflects at that point — a dark cabinet, a lit countertop, a window, a person. The panel adds photons and can subtract none, which removes every conventional legibility device at once. No scrim behind text. No dark card under a block. No shadow, no plate, no overlay. Anything drawn to improve contrast is itself emitted light, so it lifts the background rather than lowering it, and it costs mirror.

Demonstrated rather than argued. A photograph of the kiosk at three feet, then a second photograph from the same position with the photographer's body interposed between the glass and the room's backlight. The rendering is byte-identical between them.

Where the body blocks the reflection the ground goes properly black and the type is crisp. Six inches away, over reflected cabinetry and counter, the same type at the same size and colour is mush.

Two things follow, and they are the whole design brief. Contrast is not a property of the stylesheet — a check comparing computed text colour against computed background would score both halves of that photograph 21:1 and never see the failure. And the only lever is emitted light per character: how bright, how large, how thick.

A fifth photograph, taken with every light in the room switched on, is the condition the design must actually be sized against. In it the reflected doorway is visibly brighter than the display's grey tiers — there is no contrast ratio to compute, because the background is outranking the foreground. The dimmest tier is not degraded there; it is absent.

The reflection is also detailed, and that is a second mechanism. Cabinet edges, door panels, chair backs, a picture frame. Thin 15px text occupies roughly the same spatial frequency as that clutter, so the two interfere and the glyphs dissolve into the scene; the large forms survive the identical background because they sit at a scale the room does not contain. Content has to differ from the room in scale, not only in brightness — which makes tightening the type the exact wrong response to a legibility problem.

Where the bright reflection falls is luck. It moves with the room, the hour and where the viewer stands, and in the all-lights photograph it lands squarely on the six-park stack — the smallest, thinnest and most numerous text on the display. Nothing in the design can rely on placement.

The ink has one value

the ramp is retired, not compressed

The deployed design carries five luminance steps: #fff, #aaa, #999, #666, and a dimmed seconds counter. On a monitor that is a legitimate hierarchy. On a mirror it is a hierarchy of how soon a line disappears, because a dimmed glyph is legible against dark reflection and absent against bright reflection, and nothing in the software knows which one is behind it.

Content-bearing text emits at maximum luminance. There is no second step.

Hierarchy is carried instead by the three things that survive an uncontrolled background:

  • Size — established independently by the legibility floor below.
  • Weight — a contrast mechanism here rather than a style choice, because stroke thickness is literally photons per character. The deployed design sets the clock and every ride name at font-weight: 300, which is the worst available choice for this medium: a hairline face over an uncontrolled ground, and thin strokes are the first thing a reflection swallows.
  • Position — what a region is, established by where it sits rather than by a label whispering at half brightness.
  • Scale separation — the reflected room supplies its own detail at roughly the frequency of small thin type, so content must sit at a scale the room does not contain. This is the one that forbids solving a space problem by shrinking the type: smaller text does not merely get harder to read, it moves toward the reflection rather than away from it.

The corollary is a good filter for the rebuild: if a datum does not warrant full luminance, it does not warrant space on a mirror. Dimming something is not a way to include it cheaply — it is a way to include it unreliably while still spending the mirror.

The legibility floor

calibrated to the owner's eyes

ISO 9241-303 recommends 16 arcminutes minimum and 20–22 comfortable. It is the wrong yardstick — those figures describe sustained reading of continuous text at a workstation. Two reports from the deployed kiosk set the real floor, and the model reproduces both to within a few percent.

At 14 ft the clock reads at 15.6′ and nothing else exceeds 7.2′, which is exactly what the owner reports. At 6 ft the smallest tier is 8.4′ and is called viewable — "fairly", which reads as the edge. So the floor sits near 10 arcminutes, and the design distance is 6 ft nominal, 9 ft outer bound.

Stated model-free so it survives any uncertainty in panel geometry: at the deployed panel and 6 ft, 1.85% of display height reads comfortably and 1.39% is marginal. Expressed as a fraction of display height the floor is resolution-independent — it survives a 4K panel and an overscan change without a second set of figures, which the deployed design cannot do, its sizes being fixed pixels identical at 1024×768 and 3840×2160.

A photograph from 9 ft confirms the outer bound is a genuine edge rather than a comfortable margin: the weather block is about all that survives, and it carries the largest type on the screen.

The mirror is the product

measured, then photographed

The largest contiguous unlit rectangle in the deployed layout is 940×939px — a 401×401 mm square, 42.6% of the surface. Lit modules cover only 23%. The photographs confirm what that buys: a bright, sharp reflection of a person and the room behind them, at conversational distance.

And the reserve is computable rather than a matter of taste. A plane mirror of extent E shows an object of extent 2E, independent of viewing distance — so 401 mm of reserve shows 802 mm of a person, head to roughly the waist. A check can measure it from module geometry; the threshold is a decision about how much of a person the product intends to show.

What the two constraints do together

density becomes arithmetic

Legibility sets a floor under every element. The mirror sets a ceiling over all of them. Between them, how much this display can carry stops being a matter of taste — though the answer is roomier than a first pass suggested.

The direction is forced by the optics rather than chosen: the mirror wants vertical extent, because that is what shows a person's height. Content therefore belongs in side columns rather than a full-width band — which means the two constraints trade against each other in width, and width is the dimension the mirror has to spare.

The longest label on the display, Hagrid's Magical Creatures Motorbike Adventure™, inks 396px today and 667px at a legible size in a heavier weight. A row carrying it needs roughly 620px including its wait-time column. A reserve sized for head and shoulders — half a shoulder span, about 550px — leaves 625px columns on both sides. It fits, with little to spare.

Label length is a budget, not a prohibition. Long names cost column width; column width comes out of mirror width, which is the slack dimension. What they do not cost is mirror height, which is what determines how much of a person the glass returns.

An earlier draft of this page asserted that a 46-character label could not exist on this display. That was wrong twice: the 520px it projected from was the module's box rather than the string, inflating the requirement by 40%, and it treated the 940px reserve that happens to exist today as though it were the obligation. The record is kept here because a confident geometric impossibility is exactly the kind of claim that survives unexamined into a rebuild.

Independent of any of this: today's layout leaves a 200px gap between the clock and the top of the park stack, which is one module of headroom. A seventh park, or the compliments module the roster includes and this configuration omits, collides with the clock — the same failure already reproducible at 1280×800.

What belongs in the specification

properties, not this design

Nothing on this page is a requirement. The type scale, the composition, the weights and the reserve size are one worked answer; a rebuild should be free to discard all of them. What the tree owes is the properties they satisfy, with every threshold living in the check that measures it.

  • The surface is a mirror wherever it carries no information — a new need, not a decomposition of the layout need. A display can render every module perfectly on a white ground and have destroyed the product. Beneath it: the ground emits nothing, and a contiguous unlit region of sufficient extent survives every configuration.
  • Content-bearing text emits at the display's maximum luminance. The reflected room sits outside the system boundary, as the kiosk host does, so the obligation is on what the software controls — its own emission. Readable from computed styles.
  • Text meets a minimum size as a fraction of display height, so the guarantee survives a change of panel or resolution.
  • Content stays within a safe-area inset, which also subsumes the overflow collision: a stack growing upward meets the inset before it meets the clock.

All four are settled by a machine, unattended, from a rendered page — which is the finding that matters most for the ticket. The obligation was never a judgement obligation needing a new home; it was three or four decidable clauses nobody had written down. The residue that genuinely cannot be verified — whether the result is handsome — is preference rather than obligation, and belongs recorded as correctly absent.
